October 28, 2026 Federal Reserve issues FOMC statement For release at 2:00 p.m. EDT Share --> --> --> --> --> --> The Federal Open Market Committee approved the following statement for release by a 10 – 2 vote: The Committee decided to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent, in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate. The Committee is continuing its policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking system. Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace. While uncertainty remains elevated owing, in part, to geopolitical developments, domestic spending has been resilient. Productivity growth is strong, and capital investment is robust. Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little. Inflation remains elevated. The accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2 percent goal. The Committee will deliver price stability. Voting against the monetary policy action were Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack, who preferred to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point at this meeting. For media inquiries, please email [email protected] or call 202-452-2955. statement Press Release October 28, 2026 Implementation Note issued October 28, 2026 Decisions Regarding Monetary Policy Implementation The Federal Reserve has made the following decisions to implement the monetary policy stance announced by the Federal Open Market Committee in its statement on October 28, 2026: The Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System voted unanimously to maintain the interest rate paid on reserve balances at 3.90 percent, effective October 29, 2026. As part of its policy decision, the Federal Open Market Committee voted to direct the Open Market Desk at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, until instructed otherwise, to execute transactions in the System Open Market Account in accordance with the following domestic policy directive: "Effective October 29, 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee directs the Desk to: Undertake open market operations as necessary to maintain the federal funds rate in a target range of 3-3/4 to 4 percent. Conduct standing overnight repurchase agreement operations at a rate of 4.0 percent. Conduct standing overnight reverse repurchase agreement operations at an offering rate of 3.75 percent and with a per-counterparty limit of $160 billion per day. When appropriate, increase the System Open Market Account holdings of securities through purchases of Treasury bills and, if needed, other Treasury securities with remaining maturities of 3 years or less to maintain an ample level of reserves. Roll over at auction all principal payments from the Federal Reserve's holdings of Treasury securities. Reinvest all principal payments from the Federal Reserve's holdings of agency securities into Treasury bills." In a related action, the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System voted unanimously to approve the establishment of the primary credit rate at the existing level of 4.0 percent. This information will be updated as appropriate to reflect decisions of the Federal Open Market Committee or the Board of Governors regarding details of the Federal Reserve's operational tools and approach used to implement monetary policy. More information regarding open market operations and reinvestments may be found on the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's website . Transcript of Chairman Warsh’s Press Conference
October 28, 2026
CHAIRMAN WARSH. Good day.
This morning the FOMC decided to maintain the target rate for the federal funds rate at
3¾ to 4 percent, in support of the Federal Reserve’s dual mandate.
Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace. While uncertainty remains—owing, in
part, to geopolitical developments—domestic spending has been resilient. Productivity growth is
strong, and capital investment robust. Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the
unemployment rate has changed little.
But inflation remains elevated. The Committee believes that the accumulation of more
information will help it determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of
inflation to the 2 percent goal. The Committee will deliver price stability.
At each policy meeting, my colleagues and I assess the economy by looking out the
window—it’s what we call “interrogating reality”—to gauge what’s really going on. It is not a
task that has grown easier.
So what exactly do we observe? Economic growth has strengthened. The GDP increase
in the first half of this year was revised up from an earlier estimate of 1.8 percent up to 2.4
percent. And the third quarter appears to have brought an even more rapid advance, led by
healthy consumer spending, likely running above 3 percent growth, and business investment,
likely rising at about a double-digit pace.
Capital expenditures are at a historically high share of GDP. High-tech investment is
leading the acceleration. But there’s also been a broadening in other categories. And these
trends bear watching.

What about the jobless rate? The jobless rate is lower than a year ago, and payroll
growth is noticeably above last year’s pace. The four-week moving average of unemployment
insurance claims continues to run at the lowest level in generations. So on the employment side
of the Fed’s dual mandate, conditions are quite positive.
And yet Americans, of course, are still dealing with inflation that has been running above
the Committee’s objective for close to 6 years. It’s our responsibility to get inflation down.
That’s the reason the FOMC’s discussion over the last couple of days focused mainly on the
price stability side of our remit.
Since our policy action in September, we have started to see some changes in financial
and credit conditions. They have tightened for some businesses and households, much as we
would expect. Market participants are becoming somewhat more discerning in their capital
allocations. And while overall financial conditions are tighter, I’m unwilling and unable to call
them tight. But broadly, these developments go in the direction of achieving a longer, more
durable period of economic growth and price stability.
Over the last couple of days, the Committee had a good discussion of the changes in the
economic and financial landscape that marked this inter-meeting period. We discussed
geopolitical developments and fast-moving changes in technology. We observed—but were not
surprised—that financial markets showed bouts of volatility and shifting correlations.
So as we widen the frame to survey the broad economic and financial picture, real
activity is clearly pointing in the right direction. That’s good news. But inflation is too high.
Energy and related prices have moved higher again in recent weeks. And our job is to ensure
that relative price changes in some sectors do not broaden out across the economy, and that
inflation expectations remain as they are anchored.

While these uncertainties are real, there’s no lack of clarity in our purpose at the Fed.
Since becoming Chairman just five months ago, I’ve been honored to work with FOMC
policymakers who are unambiguous in their commitment to stable prices. We’ve made clear our
readiness to act as circumstances require, and we have shown our willingness to act.
The information we receive over the coming period will help us determine where we
want policy to be. And at every turn, good or bad, our job is to get monetary policy right. And
that’s exactly what we’re doing.
Now, with this objective in mind, we have decided on a change in our FOMC schedule,
starting next fall. We believe eight meetings per year is the right number for the FOMC. But in
2027, one meeting will focus exclusively on broad monetary policy strategy, with neither a vote
on the federal funds rate nor a policy statement anticipated. The rest will be regular FOMC
policy-setting meetings. And I expect that in 2028, we’ll have two meetings dedicated to
discussions of strategy, and six for setting policy.
Now, what’s the reasoning behind this change? To me, it’s quite straightforward.
Getting policy right involves assessing near-term developments at timely, regular, and
information-rich intervals. But it’s equally important we reserve time and space, as an
intellectually diverse group, for strategic deliberations—for thinking anew about big questions,
looking out over longer time horizons, focusing, as I say, on the left side of the decimal point.
I believe the setting of the meeting schedule and agenda is more than some administrative
detail. It’s an institutional choice, affecting the nature and the quality of our deliberations, and,
ultimately, the wisdom of our decisions. It’s yet another change to get the conduct of monetary
policy on the right track.
